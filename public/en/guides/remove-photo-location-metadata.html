How to remove the location (GPS) and metadata from a photo

Updated: July 2026 · Reading time: 5 minutes · By Gabriel Maglia

Every photo you take with your phone stores, inside the file, data you don't see: the device model, the date and —most sensitive— the exact GPS location where you took it. That's the EXIF metadata, and it travels with the photo when you share it. This guide explains why it matters and how to remove the location before publishing or sending an image, without uploading it anywhere.

View and remove the metadata →

Why it matters (more than it seems)

A seemingly innocent photo —your pet, something you're selling, your car— can carry your home address inside if you took it there. If you post it on a classifieds site, send it to a stranger, or upload it to a site that doesn't strip metadata, you're sharing your location without realizing. For anyone selling online, taking orders or simply caring about privacy, this is a real risk that's easy to avoid.

What data a photo stores

  • GPS location: latitude and longitude of where it was taken (the most sensitive data).
  • Device: make and model of the camera or phone.
  • Date and time: the exact moment of capture.
  • Settings: shot parameters and, sometimes, the software it was edited with.

Step by step to remove it

  1. Open the photo in the metadata viewer.
  2. Check what it carries: it tells you if it includes GPS location.
  3. Tap "Download without metadata" to get a clean copy.
  4. Share that copy, not the original.

Don't the networks strip it automatically?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. Big networks usually remove metadata on publish, but not all of them and not always — and if you send the original photo by email, by uncompressed messaging, or upload it to any site, the location travels with it. Relying on "the network will strip it" is not a strategy; removing it yourself before sharing is.

What happens to quality

Removing metadata regenerates the photo without the hidden data, and for that it gets recompressed: in my test the copy's pixels were no longer identical to the original's and the file went from 21.0 to 24.1 KB. I didn't measure how noticeable that is by eye. What it does stop carrying is your location, the device model and the date.

Do it without uploading your photo

It would be absurd to upload your photo to someone else's server to protect your privacy. In PhotoCut everything happens in your browser: reading the metadata and the clean copy are done locally, and the image isn't uploaded anywhere.

We tested it: which EXIF fields the panel shows and what stays in the clean copy

Image: gps-sintetica.jpg, 1600 × 1200 px, 21 KB (synthetic image I drew with Pillow).

Tool: File → “Download without metadata”.

What I did:

  1. With Pillow I generated a 1600 × 1200 px JPG with test EXIF: make, model, software, date and the GPS coordinates of a public monument.
  2. I opened it in the “File” tab and read what the “Camera & capture (EXIF)” section shows.
  3. I pressed “Download without metadata” and opened the result with Pillow to count the EXIF tags and the GPS block.

What I measured:

  • The original has 4 main EXIF fields (make, model, software, date), 1 in the capture block and 4 GPS fields.
  • What the panel showed (my session ran in the Spanish interface; here I give the labels the way the English interface names them): “Camera & capture (EXIF) Make PhotoCut Pruebas Model Camara Sintetica 1 Capture date 2026:10:06 12:00:00 Software gen-inputs.py ⚠ This photo includes your GPS location. Anyone with the file can see it. GPS -34.6037, -58.3816”.
  • The copy without metadata: 0 EXIF fields, 0 GPS fields, 1600 × 1200 px and 24.1 KB (the original weighed 21.0 KB). Download in 0.1 s. It does carry an ICC color profile that wasn't in the original.
  • The copy's pixels aren't identical to the original's: the file is re-encoded with loss (JPEG).

What went wrong or what limit I saw: The photo is synthetic and I put the GPS in myself: I didn't test real phone photos, or formats such as HEIC, or other fields that can remain (embedded thumbnail, maker notes). The clean copy is recompressed (it weighs more and the pixels aren't identical) and carries an ICC color profile the original didn't have.

Tested by Gabriel Maglia on 2026-10-06 with PhotoCut (web) in Chrome 154.0.8037.98 · macOS 14.8.8 · PhotoCut version: commit bc142c7.
